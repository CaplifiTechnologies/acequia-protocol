# Ditch AI

## A Community Headgate for Data Infrastructure

---

### What Ditch AI Is

Ditch AI is an open standards body that governs the Acequia Protocol — a specification for community-controlled data infrastructure modeled on the acequia irrigation commons of the American Southwest.

The core premise is simple. In acequia governance, nothing flows without community authorization, every diversion is measured, and the ditch belongs to the people who depend on it. Ditch AI applies the same architecture to data: collection requires a community vote, access requires auditable authorization, and the infrastructure itself is held by civic institutions — not vendors, not platforms, not governments acting unilaterally.

Ditch AI publishes open specifications, certifies compliant operators, and maintains governance standards. It does not deliver services, hold data, or collect service revenue. This separation is structural, not aspirational — the entity that sets the rules cannot also be the entity that profits from interpreting them.

Ditch AI is named for what it protects. A ditch is unglamorous infrastructure that a community depends on completely. The name signals institutional values — maintenance over disruption, stewardship over extraction, commons over enclosure.

---

### Where This Comes From

The founding story of the American Southwest isn't the Boston Tea Party. It's farmers in an arid landscape who understood that water is life, that there isn't enough for everyone if anyone hoards, and that the only durable solution is shared infrastructure with shared governance.

Nobody in the San Luis Valley in 1852 was debating political theory. They were digging a ditch because their families needed to eat.

The acequia system that emerged wasn't designed by a government. It was designed by neighbors. The ditch was built by the people who used it. The mayordomo was chosen by the people who depended on it. The rules weren't imposed from above — they were negotiated face to face by people who had to live with each other after the meeting ended.

The core principle was simple: the water belongs to the ditch, the ditch belongs to the community, and nobody — not the richest farmer, not the territorial government, not a speculator from back east — gets to sever the resource from the people who depend on it.

This worked. Not as theory — as operational infrastructure, running continuously for centuries, across Spanish colonial governance, Mexican sovereignty, American territorial status, and statehood. Administrations changed. Legal regimes changed. The ditch kept running because the governance was local, the infrastructure was physical, and the community's dependency made the institution unkillable.

### The Acequia Protocol

When a municipality operates surveillance cameras, license plate readers, or sensor networks in public spaces, the data generated belongs to the same category as the roads those cameras sit on — public infrastructure, funded by the community, operating in the community's interest.

The Acequia Protocol establishes that community-generated data is civic infrastructure. The architecture is the governance:

**Community Authorization.** If a municipality deploys surveillance or sensor infrastructure in public spaces, the community votes on it. Collection requires consent — not the buried-in-terms-of-service kind, but the show-up-at-a-meeting-and-be-counted kind. The community decides what is collected, for how long, and under what conditions.

**The Headgate.** A deterministic gate sits between collection and access. Every request for community data passes through a controlled aperture. The gate enforces the access policies the community has authorized. It opens for legitimate, scoped queries. It stays closed for everything else.

**Measurement.** Every access is logged in an append-only audit ledger. Who accessed what, when, under what authorization, for what stated purpose. The community can audit these logs at any time. Measurement is not optional — it is the mechanism that makes governance real rather than aspirational.

**Civic Custody.** The physical data lives in a civic institution. The Acequia Protocol specifies the local library as the institutional home for community data infrastructure. Libraries are publicly funded, locally governed, and carry a century-plus institutional tradition of custodial independence. A library cannot be acquired. It has no shareholders. It has no fiduciary duty to maximize extraction.

**Appurtenance.** Community-generated data is appurtenant to the community that generated it. It cannot be severed and sold. This is not a contractual "do not sell" flag — it is structural. The data physically lives in an institution that has no mechanism to sell it, no board that could vote to sell it, and a professional class with an ethical tradition of refusing to cooperate with extraction even when legally pressured.

### How It Handles Investigations

The Acequia Protocol is a gate, not a wall. Legitimate investigations require access to community data, and the headgate accommodates this.

**Warrant-based access.** A detective with a warrant can query the system. The headgate opens for specific, scoped, authorized requests — "was this plate seen in this corridor during this window" — and logs the access. The evidence chain is clean because custody never left a governed system.

**Query goes to the data.** In a privacy-preserving integration, the investigator's query goes to the data — not the data to the investigator. A scoped query returns a scoped answer. No bulk access. No browsing. This is how the Fourth Amendment says it's supposed to work.

**Governed federation.** When an investigation spans jurisdictions, the Acequia Protocol defines governed inter-jurisdictional sharing. Town A can query Town B's headgate, but only if both communities have authorized the reciprocal arrangement, the query is scoped to an active investigation, and both audit logs record the exchange. That's mutual aid, not mutual surveillance.

**Stronger evidence.** An append-only audit ledger with civic custody creates a chain of custody that holds up in court. Every access is recorded. Integrity is verifiable. Defense challenges to evidence provenance — increasingly common — are answered by the architecture itself.

### The Three Roles

The acequia system separates governance into three distinct roles. Collapsing them breaks the model.

**The Ditch Commission — Ditch AI.** Sets governance, owns the specification, certifies compliant operators. Never touches service revenue. Funded through memberships, grants, and certification fees. Its credibility depends on structural independence from any commercial implementer.

**The Mayordomo — Certified Operators.** Managed service providers who deliver the operational layer. They operate under contracts that bind them to the Ditch AI governance spec. They are auditable, fireable, and replaceable. Any qualified operator can seek certification. Replaceability is a feature — it is what makes the community's position durable.

**The Parciantes — The Community.** The municipality, the library, and the people. They own the physical infrastructure. They vote on access policy. They choose and can replace the operator. They can close the headgate.

Most municipalities under 50,000 people don't have dedicated IT staff. They're running on vendor contracts and a part-time administrator. The mayordomo layer solves this — a community doesn't need to hire a CISO. It needs a service provider who is structurally bound to the governance model, accountable to the community, and replaceable if it fails.

### Pre-Partisan by Design

The acequia predates the Democratic Party, the Republican Party, and the United States Constitution. The Acequia Protocol is not a position on a political spectrum. It is how communities governed shared resources before anyone told them they needed a vendor to do it for them.

It protects the community from overreach in every direction — foreign and domestic, public and private. The headgate does not distinguish between threats. The question is always the same: did the community authorize this flow? Is it measured? Can they close it?

A progressive city council adopts it because it ensures public accountability. A conservative rural county adopts it because it keeps distant authorities out of local business. A libertarian sees it as limiting concentrated power regardless of its source. Same architecture, every audience, no contradiction.

### What Libraries Get

Libraries gain infrastructure investment, a renewed civic mission, and institutional relevance at a moment when most library systems are fighting for budget survival. The Acequia Protocol positions the library as essential civic data infrastructure — not a legacy institution, but the most trustworthy one.

Hosting community data infrastructure makes the library unkillable in budget conversations. It transforms the library from a place people visit into the institution that protects the community's most sensitive civic asset. That's a funding argument no book-lending metric can match.

### Summary

Your local government's job is to protect you, not to package you.

The Acequia Protocol exists because your town deserves the same governance over its data that acequia communities have maintained over their water for four hundred years. The governance pattern already exists. It's been tested for centuries. It was designed by farmers, not theorists.

The only thing missing is the specification that translates it from water to data — from an irrigation ditch in the San Luis Valley to a library server room in any American town.
